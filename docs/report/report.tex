\begin{abstract}
Methane-loaded MOF-5 heat capacity is estimated by combining classical NPT
molecular dynamics with a loaded-system harmonic quantum correction from
PET-JAX/SADMOF Hessians. PET-MAD and PET-SOL calculations at 50, 100, and
150 methane molecules give exploratory room-temperature values of about
0.93, 1.07--1.08, and \SI{1.16}{J\per g\per K}. Sampling errors and paired
last-layer prediction-rigidity (LLPR) model uncertainties are propagated
separately. Production drift, imaginary modes, incomplete relaxation, and
sensitivity to uncertainty propagation prevent a claim of convergence.
Variation across quenched minima remains unestimated. The empty-framework
Hessian provides a separate reference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scientific motivation and scope}

MOF-5 combines high-frequency bond vibrations with soft framework motion;
methane adds internal vibrations and anharmonic motion within the pores.
Classical MD samples the latter, while normal modes correct the excessive
classical occupation of high-frequency vibrations. The conventional host
cell contains 424 atoms,
$\mathrm{C}_{192}\mathrm{H}_{96}\mathrm{O}_{104}\mathrm{Zn}_{32}$;
50, 100, and 150 methane molecules increase the totals to 674, 924, and 1174.

Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarizes the calculation. All loaded terms use
the same PET checkpoint, composition, and mass normalization. The aim is to
test the hybrid approximation of Kapil \emph{et al.}\ \cite{kapil2019}
on PET through convergence and validation, rather than matching their
force-field results.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Quantum Mechanics}
\label{sec:qm}

\subsubsection{The Hamiltonian and the Schrödinger equation}

When treating a system of atoms or molecules, we need to treat them as a quantum system. If neglecting relativistic effects, the system is described by a nonrelativistic Hamiltonian, which for electron positions $\bm r$ and nuclear positions $\bm R$ is

\begin{align}
  \hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{tot}}
    &=\hat T_{\mathrm n}+\hat T_{\mathrm e}
      +\hat V_{\mathrm{ee}}+\hat V_{\mathrm{en}}+\hat V_{\mathrm{nn}},
      \label{eq:electron-nuclear-hamiltonian}\\
  \hat T_{\mathrm n}
    &=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_i}\nabla_{\bm R_i}^2,
  \qquad
  \hat T_{\mathrm e}
    =-\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm e}}\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}
      \nabla_{\bm r_a}^2.
\end{align}

The $\hat T$ terms represent the kinetic energy of the nuclei and electrons, respectively, while the $\hat V$ terms describe electron--electron, electron--nuclear, and nuclear--nuclear Coulomb interactions. To know the state of our quantum system, we need to use the Hamiltonian in the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, which is a partial differential equation for the wavefunction $\Psi(\bm r,\bm R,t)$:

\begin{equation}
  i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(\bm r,\bm R,t)}{\partial t}
    =\hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{tot}}\Psi(\bm r,\bm R,t).
  \label{eq:full-schrodinger}
\end{equation}

The problem is that solving this equation analytically is only possible for the simplest system, the hydrogen atom.
For any other atom or molecule, there is no analytical solution and we have to rely on approximations and numerical methods.
All approximations are not created equal, we have to justify using them in each case and we have to think about their feasability.
The Born--Oppenheimer approximation \cite{born1927} is one of the most important approximations in quantum chemistry and molecular physics, and it is the one we will use in this work.

\subsubsection{The Born--Oppenheimer approximation}
\label{sec:bo}

Because nuclei are much heavier than electrons, their motion is much slower than that of the electrons.
As the nuclei move along the PES, the electrons adjust instantaneously to the new nuclear positions,
which allows us to separate the motion of the nuclei from the motion of the electrons.
In other words, we can solve the electronic part of the Schrödinger equation using the electronic Hamiltonian

\begin{align}
  \hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{el}}
    &=\hat T_{\mathrm e}+\hat V_{\mathrm{ee}}+\hat V_{\mathrm{en}}
    \label{eq:bo-potential}
\end{align}

by considering the nuclear positions as fixed parameters in the time-independent Schrödinger equation

\begin{align}
  \hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{el}}(\bm R)
    \psi_0(\bm r;\bm R)
    &=\varepsilon_0(\bm R)\psi_0(\bm r;\bm R),
    \label{eq:electronic-ground-state}\\
\end{align}

Solving this equation gives us the electronic ground-state wavefunction $\psi_0(\bm r;\bm R)$ and the electronic energy $\varepsilon_0(\bm R)$,
which depends on the nuclear positions parametrically.
Using the nuclear Hamiltonian

\begin{align}
  \hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{nuc}}
    =-\sum_i\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_i}\nabla_{\bm R_i}^2+U(\bm R),
    \qquad
    U(\bm R)=\varepsilon_0(\bm R)+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm R)
    \label{eq:bo-hamiltonian}
\end{align}

we can solve the nuclear part of the Schrödinger equation

\begin{align}
  \hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{nuc}}\chi(\bm R)
    &=E_n\chi(\bm R),
    \label{eq:bo-nuclear-schrödinger}
\end{align}

Solving this equation gives us the nuclear wavefunction $\chi(\bm R)$ and the nuclear energy $E_n$.
The total wavefunction of the system can then be approximated as the product of the electronic and nuclear wavefunctions:

\begin{equation}
  \Psi(\bm r,\bm R)
    \approx\psi_0(\bm r;\bm R)\chi(\bm R),
    \label{eq:bo-total-wavefunction}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Classical MD and quantum nuclear statistics}

Classical MD further replaces nuclear operators by positions and momenta,
giving the Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:classical-hamiltonian} and Newtonian
forces $m_i\ddot{\bm R}_i=-\nabla_iU$. A useful harmonic criterion and
thermal length scale are
\begin{equation}
  \beta\hbar\omega\ll1,
  \qquad
  \lambda_i=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m_i k_{\mathrm B}T}},
  \qquad \beta=(k_{\mathrm B}T)^{-1}.
  \label{eq:nuclear-classical-limit}
\end{equation}
Classical sampling is appropriate when quantum level spacing is small
relative to thermal energy and nuclear delocalization is negligible on
relevant structural scales. A hydrogen-containing system can satisfy these
conditions for slow collective modes while violating them for bond vibrations.

Retaining quantum nuclei on the same surface gives the canonical density
operator, partition function, and observable average
\begin{align}
  \hat\rho_{\mathrm q}
    &=Z_{\mathrm q}^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{BO}}},
  \qquad
  Z_{\mathrm q}=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{BO}}}
    =\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n},
    \label{eq:quantum-nuclear-statistics}\\
  \langle A\rangle_{\mathrm q}
    &=\operatorname{Tr}(\hat\rho_{\mathrm q}\hat A).
\end{align}
The nuclear trace avoids explicit electronic propagation, but not the
quantum nuclear problem. PIMD evaluates it through imaginary-time paths:
factorizing the exponential and inserting position states produces the
ring-polymer distribution in Section~\ref{sec:pimd-theory}.

We will use the Born--Oppenheimer surface for classical MD and also for
PIMD to include nuclear quantum effects, especially in $\mathrm{H_2O}$
loadings. Hydrogen's small mass increases delocalization; O--H zero-point
motion and quantum fluctuations of hydrogen bonds motivate this treatment
\cite{ceriotti2013water}. Their effect on confined-water heat capacity must
be evaluated on the selected potential. PIMD retains anharmonic nuclear
sampling, rather than adding a harmonic correction to classical motion.
The methane results reported here still use classical MD with the separate
harmonic correction; water PIMD is a planned extension.

\subsection{Density Functional Theory}
\label{sec:dft}

At fixed nuclear positions, density functional theory (DFT) expresses the
electronic ground-state energy in terms of the electron density
$n(\bm r)$ rather than the many-electron wavefunction. This provides the
Born--Oppenheimer potential-energy surface used to generate reference
energies, forces, and stresses for machine-learned potentials.

\subsubsection{The Hohenberg--Kohn theorems}

For a fixed electron number $N_{\mathrm e}$ and external potential
$v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm r;\bm R)$, the electronic Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{el}}
    =\hat T_{\mathrm e}+\hat V_{\mathrm{ee}}
      +\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm e}}v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm r_a;\bm R).
  \label{eq:dft-electronic-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
The Hohenberg--Kohn (HK) theorems establish the basis of ground-state DFT
\cite{hohenberg1964}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The ground-state density $n_0(\bm r)$ determines the external
    potential up to an additive constant, and hence the electronic
    Hamiltonian and ground-state properties.
  \item The ground-state energy is the minimum of the density functional
    $E_v[n]$, attained at $n_0$, over admissible densities with
    $\int n(\bm r)\,\mathrm d\bm r=N_{\mathrm e}$.
\end{enumerate}
Writing the universal functional $F[n]$ in constrained-search form makes
this variational principle explicit \cite{martin}:
\begin{align}
  F[n]&=\min_{\psi\to n}
    \langle\psi|\hat T_{\mathrm e}+\hat V_{\mathrm{ee}}|\psi\rangle,\\
  E_v[n]&=F[n]+\int v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm r;\bm R)n(\bm r)\,\mathrm d\bm r,
  \qquad E_0=\min_{n\ \mathrm{admissible}}E_v[n].
  \label{eq:hk-variational-principle}
\end{align}
HK therefore turns the electronic problem into a density minimization,
but does not supply a practical expression for $F[n]$.

\subsubsection{From HK to the Kohn--Sham equations}

Kohn--Sham (KS) theory introduces an auxiliary system of noninteracting
electrons with the same density \cite{kohn1965}. Its orthonormal orbitals
$\phi_a$ and occupations $f_a$ give
\begin{equation}
  n(\bm r)=\sum_a f_a|\phi_a(\bm r)|^2,
  \qquad \sum_a f_a=N_{\mathrm e}.
  \label{eq:kohn-sham-density}
\end{equation}
The occupations include spin multiplicity; for a periodic solid the sum
also includes band indices and wavevector weights. The unknown HK
functional is decomposed as
\begin{align}
  F[n]&=T_{\mathrm s}[n]+E_{\mathrm H}[n]+E_{\mathrm{xc}}[n],\\
  T_{\mathrm s}[n]&=\sum_a f_a\int\phi_a^*(\bm r)
    \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla^2\right)
    \phi_a(\bm r)\,\mathrm d\bm r,\\
  E_{\mathrm H}[n]&=\frac12\iint n(\bm r)w(\bm r,\bm r')n(\bm r')
    \,\mathrm d\bm r\,\mathrm d\bm r'.
  \label{eq:dft-ks-decomposition}
\end{align}
Here $T_{\mathrm s}$ is the noninteracting kinetic energy and
$E_{\mathrm H}$ is the classical electron--electron electrostatic energy.
For the periodic MOF, $w$ is the periodic Coulomb kernel.
The exchange--correlation functional $E_{\mathrm{xc}}$ contains the
remaining interaction energy and the correction to $T_{\mathrm s}$.
Thus the electronic energy functional becomes
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{el}}[n;\bm R]
    =T_{\mathrm s}[n]+\int v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm r;\bm R)n(\bm r)
      \,\mathrm d\bm r+E_{\mathrm H}[n]+E_{\mathrm{xc}}[n].
  \label{eq:dft-energy-functional}
\end{equation}
Minimizing this energy with respect to the orbitals while preserving their
orthonormality gives the one-electron KS equations:
\begin{align}
  \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla^2
    +v_{\mathrm{eff}}[n](\bm r)\right]\phi_a(\bm r)
    &=\epsilon_a\phi_a(\bm r),
    \label{eq:kohn-sham-equations}\\
  v_{\mathrm{eff}}[n](\bm r)
    &=v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm r)+v_{\mathrm H}[n](\bm r)
      +v_{\mathrm{xc}}[n](\bm r),
    \label{eq:kohn-sham-potential}\\
  v_{\mathrm H}[n](\bm r)
    &=\int w(\bm r,\bm r')n(\bm r')\,\mathrm d\bm r',
  \qquad v_{\mathrm{xc}}[n](\bm r)
    =\frac{\delta E_{\mathrm{xc}}}{\delta n(\bm r)}.
    \label{eq:ks-functional-derivatives}
\end{align}
The decomposition is formally exact when the auxiliary system can
reproduce the density; practical DFT approximates $E_{\mathrm{xc}}$.
Because $v_{\mathrm{eff}}$ depends on $n$, the equations are solved
self-consistently: start from a trial density, solve for the orbitals,
rebuild the density, and iterate until both density and energy converge.

At the converged density $n_0$, adding nuclear repulsion gives the
potential energy and its derivatives give the reference forces:
\begin{equation}
  U_{\mathrm{DFT}}(\bm R)
    =E_{\mathrm{el}}[n_0;\bm R]+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm R),
  \qquad
  \bm F_i^{\mathrm{DFT}}
    =-\left.\nabla_{\bm R_i}U_{\mathrm{DFT}}\right|_{\bm h}.
  \label{eq:dft-reference-energy-forces}
\end{equation}
Cell-strain derivatives similarly provide stresses. These quantities
supply training labels for MLIPs such as PET-MAD \cite{petmad}. In this
project, the MLIP then evaluates the potential-energy surface during MD
and Hessian calculations without repeating the electronic calculation.

\subsection{Statistical Mechanics}
\label{sec:sm}

The classical simulations in this project use the isothermal--isobaric
($NPT$) ensemble. The numbers of framework atoms and guest molecules are
fixed, while the system exchanges energy and volume with reservoirs at
temperature $T$ and imposed hydrostatic pressure $P$.
Allowing the periodic cell to fluctuate captures the volume response of
the loaded framework. The quantity of interest is therefore the
constant-pressure heat capacity at fixed loading
\cite{allen2017,tuckerman2023}.

For $N$ classical atoms, write the positions and momenta explicitly as
$\bm r^N=(\bm r_1,\ldots,\bm r_N)$ and
$\bm p^N=(\bm p_1,\ldots,\bm p_N)$.
The cell dependence of functions is left implicit throughout this section:
they are evaluated in the instantaneous periodic cell, with $V=\det\bm h$.
The cell remains an independent fluctuating variable; positions and momenta
alone do not determine its volume. We denote the physical Hamiltonian by
$E$, reserving $H$ for enthalpy:
\begin{equation}
  E(\bm r^N,\bm p^N)
    =K(\bm p^N)+U(\bm r^N)
    =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|\bm p_i|^2}{2m_i}+U(\bm r^N),
  \label{eq:classical-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where $U$ is the MLIP potential energy. The instantaneous physical
enthalpy is
\begin{equation}
  H(\bm r^N,\bm p^N)
    =E(\bm r^N,\bm p^N)+PV
    =\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|\bm p_i|^2}{2m_i}+U(\bm r^N)+PV.
  \label{eq:physical-enthalpy}
\end{equation}
It uses the imposed pressure and excludes thermostat and barostat energies.

With $\beta=(k_{\mathrm B}T)^{-1}$, the NPT partition function and
probability density are
\begin{align}
  \Delta(N,P,T)
    &=\mathcal C\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm dV}{V_0}
       \int_{\mathrm{cell}(V)}\mathrm d^{3N}r
       \int\mathrm d^{3N}p\,
       e^{-\beta H(\bm r^N,\bm p^N)},
       \label{eq:npt-partition-function}\\
  \rho(\bm r^N,\bm p^N)
    &=\Delta^{-1}e^{-\beta H(\bm r^N,\bm p^N)}.
       \label{eq:npt-density}
\end{align}
Here $V_0$ is a fixed, temperature- and pressure-independent reference
volume, and $\mathcal C$ contains the fixed phase-space normalization
factors needed to make $\Delta$ dimensionless.
The position integral covers the instantaneous cell for every atom, and
the momentum integral covers the allowed momenta.
The volume integral is retained because the cell fluctuates in NPT.
Although the cell argument is suppressed in $\rho$, this is the joint
density of atomic positions, momenta, and cell volume, rather than a
density with the volume already integrated out. Every equilibrium
observable is averaged as

\begin{equation}
  \langle A\rangle
    =\mathcal C\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm dV}{V_0}
       \int_{\mathrm{cell}(V)}\mathrm d^{3N}r
       \int\mathrm d^{3N}p\,
       \rho(\bm r^N,\bm p^N)A(\bm r^N,\bm p^N),
  \label{eq:ensemble-average}
\end{equation}

Throughout this report, $P$ denotes the imposed hydrostatic pressure,
and equilibrium averages, variances, and covariances refer to the NPT
ensemble unless stated otherwise. Ensemble subscripts are omitted.

The corresponding thermodynamic potential is the Gibbs free energy.
For a temperature-independent Hamiltonian,
\begin{align}
  G&=-k_{\mathrm B}T\ln\Delta, &
  \langle H\rangle
    &=-\left(\frac{\partial\ln\Delta}{\partial\beta}\right)_{N,P},\\
  \langle V\rangle
    &=\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\right)_{N,T}, &
  S&=-\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_{N,P},\\
  \langle E\rangle
    &=\langle H\rangle-P\langle V\rangle, &
  G&=\langle H\rangle-TS.
\end{align}

The volume integral above displays the scalar-volume formulation. In the
actual simulations the cell matrix $\bm h$ also permits anisotropic strain
under hydrostatic external pressure, with $V=\det\bm h$.
Fractional coordinates $\bm s_i=\bm h^{-1}\bm r_i$ give
$\mathrm d^{3N}r=V^N\mathrm d^{3N}s$; integrating out the momenta therefore
gives a configurational weight proportional to
$V^N e^{-\beta[U+PV]}$ in fractional coordinates.
Flexible-cell sampling must include the appropriate cell measure and
extended-system corrections \cite{mtk}.

Additionally, we assume the ergodic hypothesis,
which states that the infinite time average of an observable along a trajectory is equal to its ensemble average.
So the integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ensemble-average} can be replaced by a time average along a sufficiently long trajectory:

\begin{equation}
  \langle A\rangle
  =\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}
   \int_0^\tau A\bigl(\bm r^N(t),\bm p^N(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm dt.
  \label{eq:ergodic-average}
\end{equation}

In practice, we can't run the simulation for ever,
so we need to run the simulation for long enough to ensure that the system has reached equilibrium and explored the relevant phase space.
We can then replace the integral by a finite sum over the trajectory frames, giving the time average

\begin{equation}
  \langle A\rangle
  \approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}A\bigl(\bm r^N(t_j),\bm p^N(t_j)\bigr),
  \label{eq:time-average}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Equipartition and temperature}

Each independent quadratic term contributes $k_{\mathrm B}T/2$. For $f$
active momentum degrees of freedom,
\begin{equation}
  \langle K\rangle=\frac{f}{2}k_{\mathrm B}T,\qquad
  T_{\mathrm{kin}}=\frac{2K}{f k_{\mathrm B}}.
  \label{eq:kinetic-temperature}
\end{equation}
Subtract constraints and deliberately removed momentum components from
$3N$; fixing total linear momentum gives $f=3N-3$. Auxiliary reservoir
variables do not count. Initial velocities follow the Maxwell--Boltzmann distribution:

\begin{equation}
  v_{i\alpha}\sim\mathcal N\!\left(0,\frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{m_i}\right),
  \qquad \alpha\in\{x,y,z\}.
\end{equation}

Subtract center-of-mass velocity when required. The NPT momentum distribution gives
$\operatorname{var}(K)=f(k_{\mathrm B}T)^2/2$ and
$\operatorname{var}(T_{\mathrm{kin}})=2T^2/f$; repeated exact rescaling
suppresses these fluctuations.

\subsubsection{Response functions and heat capacity}
\label{sec:md-response}

For an observable $A$ with no explicit temperature dependence and a
temperature-independent Hamiltonian, differentiating the NPT average gives
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{\partial\langle A\rangle}{\partial T}\right)_{N,P}
  =\operatorname{cov}\left(A,\frac{\partial\ln\rho}{\partial T}\right)
  =\frac{\operatorname{cov}(A,H)}{k_{\mathrm B}T^2},
\end{equation}

Taking $A=H$ gives the constant-pressure heat capacity:

\begin{equation}
  C_P=\left(\frac{\partial\langle H\rangle}{\partial T}\right)_{N,P}
      =\frac{\operatorname{var}(H)}{k_{\mathrm B}T^2}.
  \label{eq:fluctuation-cp}
\end{equation}
For separable kinetic energy, the momenta are independent of the
configuration and volume. The equivalent configurational estimator is
\begin{equation}
  C_P=\frac{f}{2}k_{\mathrm B}
       +\frac{\operatorname{var}(U+PV)}{k_{\mathrm B}T^2}.
\end{equation}
The kinetic contribution $f k_{\mathrm B}/2$ must be included when using
potential-energy and volume fluctuations alone.

The volume responses are also expressed entirely through NPT averages:
\begin{align}
  \kappa_T&=-\frac{1}{\langle V\rangle}
    \left(\frac{\partial\langle V\rangle}{\partial P}\right)_{N,T}
    =\frac{\operatorname{var}(V)}{k_{\mathrm B}T\langle V\rangle},\\
  \alpha_V&=\frac{1}{\langle V\rangle}
    \left(\frac{\partial\langle V\rangle}{\partial T}\right)_{N,P}
    =\frac{\operatorname{cov}(V,H)}{k_{\mathrm B}T^2\langle V\rangle}.
\end{align}
Explicitly state-dependent effective potentials require additional
response terms. The present workflow instead differentiates mean NPT
enthalpies across temperatures (Eq.~\eqref{eq:finite-difference-cp}).
Smaller temperature spacing reduces discretization bias but amplifies noise.
Cell heat capacities become mass-specific values through
$c_P=C_P/M_{\mathrm{cell}}$, including guest mass.

\subsubsection{Quantum harmonic heat capacity and the Dulong--Petit law}
\label{sec:quantum-statistical-mechanics}

When considering quantum systems instead of classical ones, the microstates are discrete and are described by the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian.
The partition function is therefore a sum over the discrete energy levels, rather than an integral over the continuous phase space.
The probability

\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm q}
    =\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm dV}{V_0}\,
      \operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta[\hat E(\hat{\bm r}^N,\hat{\bm p}^N)+PV]},
  \qquad
  \hat\rho=\Delta_{\mathrm q}^{-1}
      e^{-\beta[\hat E(\hat{\bm r}^N,\hat{\bm p}^N)+PV]}.
\end{equation}
An average is obtained by integrating
$\operatorname{Tr}[\hat\rho\hat A]$ over $\mathrm dV/V_0$, with the cell
argument again implicit.
The enthalpy derivative therefore defines $C_P$ as before. In this project,
however, the quantum correction is evaluated at a fixed optimized cell,
where the harmonic modes have fixed frequencies. The following averages
are explicitly for this fixed-cell quantum harmonic reference, and give
$C_V$; the classical MD contribution remains at constant pressure.

Expanding the potential about a stable minimum and using mass-weighted
normal coordinates $Q_k$ gives independent harmonic oscillators:
\begin{equation}
  \hat E_{\mathrm{har}}
    =U_{\min}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal V}
      \left(\frac{\hat\Pi_k^2}{2}
        +\frac{\omega_k^2\hat Q_k^2}{2}\right),
  \qquad
  \epsilon_{k,n}=\hbar\omega_k\left(n+\frac12\right),
  \quad n=0,1,\ldots .
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal V$ contains the retained positive-frequency modes and
$\Pi_k$ is conjugate to $Q_k$. Each mode has partition function
\begin{equation}
  z_k=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta\epsilon_{k,n}}
     =\frac{e^{-x_k/2}}{1-e^{-x_k}},
  \qquad x_k=\beta\hbar\omega_k,
  \qquad Z_{\mathrm{har}}=e^{-\beta U_{\min}}\prod_{k\in\mathcal V}z_k.
  \label{eq:harmonic-partition-function}
\end{equation}
Differentiating its logarithm gives the mean energy:
\begin{equation}
  \langle E\rangle_{\mathrm{har}}
    =-\frac{\partial\ln Z_{\mathrm{har}}}{\partial\beta}
    =U_{\min}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal V}\hbar\omega_k
      \left(\frac12+\frac{1}{e^{x_k}-1}\right).
  \label{eq:harmonic-mean-energy}
\end{equation}
The zero-point energy $\hbar\omega_k/2$ is independent of temperature at
fixed frequencies and does not contribute to the heat capacity.
Differentiating the thermal occupation gives
\begin{equation}
  C_{V,\mathrm q}^{\mathrm{har}}
    =\left(\frac{\partial\langle E\rangle_{\mathrm{har}}}{\partial T}\right)_V
    =\sum_{k\in\mathcal V}k_{\mathrm B}
       \frac{x_k^2e^{x_k}}{(e^{x_k}-1)^2}.
  \label{eq:harmonic-quantum-derivation}
\end{equation}
Modes with $\hbar\omega_k\gg k_{\mathrm B}T$ are thermally suppressed,
whereas low-frequency modes approach their classical contribution.

In the high-temperature limit, $x_k\ll1$, using
$e^{x_k}-1\simeq x_k$ gives $C_{V,k}\to k_{\mathrm B}$.
Classical equipartition gives the same result: each oscillator has one
quadratic kinetic and one quadratic potential term, together contributing
$k_{\mathrm B}T$ to its mean energy. Thus
\begin{equation}
  C_{V,\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{har}}=|\mathcal V|k_{\mathrm B},
  \qquad C_V\longrightarrow 3Nk_{\mathrm B},
  \qquad C_{V,\mathrm{molar}}\longrightarrow 3R,
  \quad R=N_{\mathrm A}k_{\mathrm B}.
  \label{eq:dulong-petit}
\end{equation}
The last two expressions are the Dulong--Petit law for a bulk solid with
$N$ atoms, with molar heat capacity defined per mole of atoms. A finite
periodic-cell calculation omitting the three uniform translations instead
has $3N-3$ vibrational modes before any additional mode filtering.
For the loaded MOF, $N$ includes both framework and guest atoms; the
mass-specific limit is $|\mathcal V|k_{\mathrm B}/M_{\mathrm{cell}}$.

The project uses these results to replace the classical harmonic
contribution by its quantum value:
\begin{equation}
  C_P^{\mathrm{hyb}}(T)\simeq C_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{anh}}(T)
    +\sum_{k\in\mathcal V}k_{\mathrm B}
      \left[\frac{x_k^2e^{x_k}}{(e^{x_k}-1)^2}-1\right].
\end{equation}
The correction vanishes in the classical high-temperature limit.
Adding this fixed-cell $C_V$ correction to the MD $C_P$ assumes that the
quantum change in $C_P-C_V$ is small.

\subsubsection{Trajectory analysis and error estimation}

When starting a molecular dynamics simulation, the system is usally not in equilibrium.
Therefore, we should spend some time running the simulation to allow the system to reach equilibrium before collecting data for analysis.
There are many quantities we can monitor to determine if the system has reached equilibrium,
such as total energy, temperature, pressure, volume, forces, density, etc.
After the system has reached equilibrium, we can start collecting data for analysis and discard the initial steps.
For each of these quantities, we can calculate the time average for each time step

\begin{equation}
  \overline A&=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}A_n,
\end{equation}

As well as the variance of the time average using the autocorrelation function of the observable $A$:

\begin{equation}
C_A(k)&=\langle(A_n-\langle A\rangle)
               (A_{n+k}-\langle A\rangle)\rangle,\\
\rho_A(k)&=C_A(k)/C_A(0).
\end{equation}

$C_A(k)$ is the autocovariance function and $\rho_A(k)$ is the autocorrelation function of the observable $A$ at time lag $k$.
The autocorrelation function measures how the values of the observable $A$ at different time steps are correlated with each other.
As the time lag $k$ increases, the correlation between the values of $A$ at different time steps decreases.
The sample-mean variance is

\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{var}(\overline A)
  =\frac{C_A(0)}{M}\left[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}
       \left(1-\frac{k}{M}\right)\rho_A(k)\right].
\end{equation}

Estimate or truncate the correlation sum statistically. Effective sample
counts depend on the observable; finer output stride does not add independent
information over a fixed duration.

Block errors should plateau for blocks longer than the correlation time,
while retaining enough blocks \cite{flyvbjerg1989}. Independent replicas
help detect slow modes, but neither method reveals a basin never visited.
Resample whole replicas or sufficiently long blocks and recompute the full
estimator, preserving cross-temperature correlations. Variance-based heat
capacities require errors of squared fluctuations, not merely errors of the
mean enthalpy. Sampling error, finite-size effects, integration bias, and
potential error need separate checks.

\subsubsection{Configurational populations}

For temperature-independent basins $\mathcal B_a$ of configuration and
cell space, their restricted NPT partition functions are
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_a=\mathcal C\int_{\mathcal B_a}
    \frac{\mathrm dV}{V_0}\,\mathrm d^{3N}r\,\mathrm d^{3N}p\,
    e^{-\beta H(\bm r^N,\bm p^N)}.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \Delta=\sum_a\Delta_a,\qquad
  w_a=\frac{\Delta_a}{\Delta},\qquad
  \langle H\rangle=\sum_a w_a\langle H\rangle_a.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  C_P=\sum_a w_a\left(\frac{\partial\langle H\rangle_a}{\partial T}\right)_{N,P}
      +\sum_a\langle H\rangle_a\left(\frac{\partial w_a}{\partial T}\right)_{N,P}.
\end{equation}
Here $\langle H\rangle_a$ is the conditional NPT mean in basin $a$.
The two terms describe motion within basins and redistribution between them.
A Hessian characterizes one local environment; adequately sampled MD also
captures changing guest arrangements and framework conformations. Basin
weights depend on accessible phase-space volume, so counting optimized
structures does not establish equilibrium populations.

\subsection{Molecular Dynamics}
\label{sec:molecular-dynamics}

\subsubsection{Classical molecular dynamics}
\label{sec:classical-md-theory}

Classical molecular dynamics (MD) generates a trajectory by integrating
the nuclear equations of motion on the MLIP potential-energy surface.
The framework and guests move together, so the trajectory samples bond
vibrations, framework deformation, guest motion, and changes of adsorption
environment. Unlike a harmonic expansion, MD evaluates the full potential
at each configuration and can therefore capture classical anharmonicity.
The role of MD here is to estimate equilibrium enthalpies at fixed loading
and pressure, rather than to solve the electronic problem at each step
\cite{allen2017,tuckerman2023}.

\paragraph{1. Specify the physical system and forces.}
For atom $i$ with mass $m_i$, position $\bm R_i$, and momentum $\bm p_i$,
the fixed-cell physical equations are
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\bm R}_i=\frac{\bm p_i}{m_i},\qquad
  \dot{\bm p}_i=\bm F_i,\qquad
  \bm F_i=-\left.\nabla_{\bm R_i}U(\bm R;\bm h)\right|_{\bm h}.
  \label{eq:classical-md-equations}
\end{equation}
The masses set the inertial response; the model supplies the potential
energy, forces, and cell derivatives. The physical energy is
$E=K+U$, where $K=\sum_i|\bm p_i|^2/(2m_i)$.
Without reservoir coupling, an exact fixed-cell trajectory conserves $E$.
The production calculation adds thermostat and barostat variables to sample
the pressure ensemble defined in Section~\ref{sec:sm}.

The chosen host structure, guest species, loading, model, and atom masses
define a state point. Guest molecules remain in the periodic system:
there is no exchange of particles with a gas reservoir. All framework and
guest atoms are flexible; molecular rotations and internal vibrations
emerge from atomic motion, without separate rigid-body equations.
The model must cover these configurations and provide mutually consistent
energies, forces, and stresses. A model with energies and forces alone is
insufficient for the flexible-cell calculation.

\paragraph{2. Represent the periodic cell and atomic neighborhoods.}
The columns of $\bm h$ are the cell vectors, $V=\det\bm h$, and fractional
coordinates obey $\bm R_i=\bm h\bm s_i$. Periodic images have positions
$\bm R_i+\bm h\bm n$, $\bm n\in\mathbb Z^3$. A periodic pair displacement is
\begin{equation}
  \bm r_{ij\bm n}=\bm R_j-\bm R_i+\bm h\bm n.
  \label{eq:md-periodic-displacement}
\end{equation}
Wrapping fractional coordinates into a unit cell changes their displayed
image, not the physical configuration. Unwrapped coordinates retain image
history and are needed when following guest displacements across boundaries.

For a single-image cutoff, $r_{\mathrm c}$ must be smaller than half the
shortest nonzero lattice-vector length; otherwise all relevant images
within the cutoff must be included. This matters for the short or skew
cells of some supplied MOFs. In a triclinic cell, rounding fractional
displacements alone need not find the geometrically nearest image.
The neighborhood construction must respect the actual cell and periodic
image labels throughout its deformation.

A neighbor list stores candidate interactions out to
$r_{\mathrm c}+r_{\mathrm{skin}}$. The skin avoids rebuilding the list after
every small displacement, while rebuild checks ensure that new interactions
are found before atoms cross into the physical cutoff. Both atomic motion
and cell deformation can alter neighbors. The current LAMMPS writer uses a
\SI{2}{\angstrom} skin and checks rebuilding every step.
Cutoffs and model derivatives must remain smooth; changing the neighbor
list must not introduce artificial force jumps. Any additional long-range
term would need its own consistent periodic treatment.

\paragraph{3. Relax the inserted structure before assigning velocities.}
Random guest insertion can leave high-energy contacts even after geometric
separation checks. Fresh loaded-system runs first minimize $U$ at fixed
cell using FIRE. This damped optimization uses auxiliary velocities and
an adaptive step to approach a local minimum; it is not a finite-temperature
trajectory \cite{lammps-fire}.
The initial cell is held fixed, so this stage relaxes atomic positions
without yet determining the equilibrium volume at the target pressure.

The input records the force tolerance, displacement bound, and iteration
and force-evaluation budgets. With the configured infinity-norm criterion,
the acceptance check is
\begin{equation}
  F_{\max}=\max_{i,\alpha}|F_{i\alpha}|\le F_{\mathrm{tol}}.
  \label{eq:initial-md-relaxation}
\end{equation}
Reaching the iteration budget alone is not convergence. Failure to meet
the force criterion, loss of atoms, or nonfinite quantities must stop the
run before thermal sampling. After FIRE, the code resets the step counter
and restores the specified MD timestep, since FIRE adapts its own step.
This initialization is distinct from the later fixed-cell quench of an
equilibrated thermal frame used to construct the harmonic reference.

\paragraph{4. Initialize the thermal momenta.}
After relaxation, fresh runs draw Gaussian atomic velocities at the target
$T$, as specified by Eq.~\eqref{eq:kinetic-temperature} and the Maxwell
variance $k_{\mathrm B}T/m_i$. The LAMMPS initialization removes net linear
and angular momentum. The seed is recorded so the initialization can be
reproduced; independent replicas should use independent placements and
velocity seeds. The temperature estimator must use the active degrees of
freedom, excluding any deliberately removed components or constraints.
Initial temperature assignment does not equilibrate the positions, volume,
or guest populations: those relax during the subsequent trajectory.

\paragraph{5. Integrate forces with a finite timestep.}
The basic fixed-cell velocity-Verlet update is
\begin{align}
  \bm p_i^{n+1/2}
    &=\bm p_i^n+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bm F_i(\bm R^n),\\
  \bm R_i^{n+1}
    &=\bm R_i^n+\Delta t\,\frac{\bm p_i^{n+1/2}}{m_i},\\
  \bm p_i^{n+1}
    &=\bm p_i^{n+1/2}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\bm F_i(\bm R^{n+1}).
  \label{eq:velocity-verlet}
\end{align}
The first half step advances the momenta using the existing force; the
position update gives the next configuration; a new model evaluation gives
the force for the final half step. This second-order, reversible,
symplectic scheme requires one new force evaluation per step once the
initial force is known. The production integrator extends this splitting
with thermostat and cell updates; the fixed-cell formulas above explain
the underlying force--motion cycle, not the complete barostatted algorithm.

The timestep must resolve the fastest motion, particularly flexible C--H
and O--H stretches. For an isolated harmonic mode, the stability condition
is $\omega_{\max}\Delta t<2$, but stability alone does not ensure accurate
enthalpies. The template uses $\Delta t=\SI{0.5}{fs}$; its adequacy requires
convergence checks. Short fixed-cell energy-conservation tests diagnose
integration bias, and comparison of enthalpies and structural observables
at smaller steps tests its effect on the final property. A thermostat can
remove numerical heating and conceal an inaccurate integrator. Adding
bond constraints would change the sampled degrees of freedom and require
a matching change to the classical and harmonic heat-capacity terms.

\paragraph{6. Couple the atoms and cell to temperature and pressure.}
The active production path uses Nos\'e--Hoover chains rather than stochastic
noise. For illustration, a single thermostat variable $\xi$ modifies
fixed-cell momenta according to
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\bm p}_i=\bm F_i-\xi\bm p_i,\qquad
  \dot\xi=\frac{2K-fk_{\mathrm B}T}{Q},\qquad
  Q\sim f k_{\mathrm B}T\tau_T^2.
\end{equation}
When $K$ is too large, the thermostat tends to increase friction; when it
is too small, it tends to restore kinetic energy. A chain thermostats the
auxiliary variables as well, improving sampling of nearly harmonic systems
\cite{nhc}. These variables control sampling but do not contribute to the
physical enthalpy. Temperature must fluctuate around its target;
forcing its instantaneous value to be constant would suppress equilibrium
fluctuations.

Pressure control requires the kinetic contribution and the configurational
virial. For unconstrained atoms with bulk streaming removed,
\begin{equation}
  \bm P_{\mathrm{inst}}
    =\frac{1}{V}\left[\sum_i\frac{\bm p_i\otimes\bm p_i}{m_i}
                         +\bm W\right],\qquad
  P_{\mathrm{inst}}=\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}\bm P_{\mathrm{inst}}.
  \label{eq:pressure-virial}
\end{equation}
Here $\otimes$ denotes an outer product. Pair forces give
$\bm W=\sum_{i<j}\bm r_{ij}\otimes\bm F_{ij}$; the many-body MLIP instead
provides the virial through strain derivatives at fixed fractional positions:
\begin{equation}
  W_{\alpha\beta}
    =-\left.\frac{\partial U}{\partial\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}}
       \right|_{\bm\epsilon=0,\{\bm s_i\}},\qquad
  \bm h\mapsto(\bm I+\bm\epsilon)\bm h.
\end{equation}
Tensile-positive stress has the opposite sign to pressure. The model's
configurational stress must be combined with the kinetic term to obtain
the full pressure \cite{lammps-pressure}.

The flexible-cell barostat responds to the difference between instantaneous
and target stress. Hydrostatic loading imposes $P\bm I$, with zero target
shear, while allowing the three lengths and three tilt factors to vary
independently. Equal normal targets therefore do not force equal strain
along each axis. At fixed fractional coordinates, a cell change carries
atoms with it through $\bm R_i=\bm h\bm s_i$; additional atomic motion
changes $\bm s_i$. The periodic geometry, neighbors, and forces must be
updated consistently as both evolve.

LAMMPS implements this through \texttt{fix npt} with \texttt{tri}, thermostat
chains on both atoms and barostat, and \texttt{mtk yes}. The latter supplies
the measure correction required for the intended pressure ensemble
\cite{mtk,lammps-npt}. The template uses a target of \SI{1}{bar}, chain length
three, and temperature/pressure damping times of 100/\SI{1000}{fs}.
These are coupling times, not the integration timestep. Fast coupling can
excite artificial oscillations; slow coupling can delay equilibration.
Energy $E$ is not conserved during reservoir exchange, and fluctuations
in temperature and instantaneous pressure are expected. Acceptance rests
on their stationary distributions and time averages, together with
structural stability.

\paragraph{7. Equilibrate, collect data, and continue interrupted runs.}
The freshly initialized system evolves under the same temperature and
pressure targets during equilibration and production. Equilibration permits
energy redistribution, volume adjustment, and guest rearrangement.
Discarding a fixed interval is a starting prescription: running averages
and block averages of enthalpy and cell parameters, framework geometry,
guest motion, and comparisons across replicas must establish stationarity.
A settled kinetic temperature alone does not demonstrate equilibration of
slow framework or adsorption modes.

The template requests $10^6$ steps, or \SI{500}{ps}, with the first
$2\times10^5$ steps (\SI{100}{ps}) assigned to equilibration. The trajectory
dump begins afterward, while the thermodynamic log also includes the early
interval. Saving every 100 steps gives \SI{0.05}{ps} spacing, but does not
make successive frames independent. The log contains $U$, $K$, temperature,
pressure components, volume, density, and cell dimensions; trajectory
frames contain atom identities, positions, velocities, forces, and cell
geometry. These complementary outputs must be matched by timestep.

Restart files preserve the dynamical state needed for continuation.
A continuation reads the latest numeric restart, skips initial relaxation
and velocity assignment, appends outputs, and advances toward the original
absolute final-step target. It is the same trajectory, not an independent
replica. Wall-time continuations extend sampling without resetting the
scientific initialization; physical or numerical failures require diagnosis.

\paragraph{8. Obtain the classical heat capacity and harmonic references.}
For each retained production sample, form the physical enthalpy
\begin{equation}
  H_n=K_n+U_n+PV_n,\qquad
  \overline H(T)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}H_n.
  \label{eq:md-physical-enthalpy-samples}
\end{equation}
Use the imposed $P$, not the fluctuating $P_{\mathrm{inst}}$, and convert
all terms to consistent energy units. Neither thermostat nor barostat
energies enter $H_n$. The mean contains both framework and guest
contributions, with fixed composition at every temperature. Repeat the
calculation over the temperature grid and estimate
\begin{equation}
  C_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{anh}}(T)
    \simeq\frac{\overline H(T+\delta T)-\overline H(T-\delta T)}{2\delta T},
  \qquad
  c_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{anh}}
    =\frac{C_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{anh}}}{M_{\mathrm{cell}}}.
  \label{eq:md-enthalpy-derivative}
\end{equation}
The default $\delta T=\SI{25}{K}$ grid runs from 175 to \SI{425}{K},
providing centered derivatives over the reported 200--\SI{400}{K} range.
Differentiate replica-averaged enthalpies when replicas are available.
Sampling errors must account for autocorrelation and be propagated through
the derivative (Section~\ref{sec:sm}); finer temperature spacing reduces
finite-difference bias but increases sensitivity to noisy means.

Finally, select representative equilibrated loaded structures for the
separate fixed-cell minimization and Hessian stage. The shared source is
\SI{400}{K} for the default reporting grid. Its modes supply the harmonic
quantum-minus-classical correction described in
Section~\ref{sec:quantum-statistical-mechanics}. The empty framework is
relaxed directly for its separate reference; it receives no MD campaign.
This division assigns classical anharmonic sampling to MD and quantum
harmonic populations to the Hessian calculation.

\subsubsection{Path integral molecular dynamics}
\label{sec:pimd-theory}

PIMD samples the quantum nuclear statistics of
Section~\ref{sec:born-oppenheimer} on the full anharmonic Born--Oppenheimer
surface. We will use it to assess quantum effects in water-loaded MOFs,
particularly those associated with hydrogen. Present methane results use
classical MD plus a harmonic correction. The following assumes distinguishable
nuclei and neglects exchange permutations \cite{tuckerman2023}.

\paragraph{Ring-polymer mapping}

Starting from Eq.~\eqref{eq:quantum-nuclear-statistics} at fixed cell,
write $\hat{\mathcal H}=\hat{\mathcal H}_{\mathrm{BO}}=\hat K+\hat U$.
The noncommuting nuclear kinetic and potential operators are split into
$L$ imaginary-time slices, $\beta_L=\beta/L$:
\begin{equation}
  e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal H}}
  =\lim_{L\to\infty}
   \left[e^{-\beta_L\hat U/2}
         e^{-\beta_L\hat K}
         e^{-\beta_L\hat U/2}\right]^L.
\end{equation}
Inserting position states gives
\begin{align}
  Z_L&=\mathcal A_L\int\prod_{j=1}^{L}\mathrm d^{3N}R^{(j)}\,
                   e^{-\beta U_L^{\mathrm{eff}}},\\
  \mathcal A_L&=\prod_{i=1}^{N}
           \left(\frac{m_iL}{2\pi\beta\hbar^2}\right)^{3L/2},\\
  U_L^{\mathrm{eff}}
    &=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}U(\bm R^{(j)};\bm h)\notag\\
    &\quad+\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i,j}\frac{m_i\omega_L^2}{2}
           |\bm R_i^{(j)}-\bm R_i^{(j+1)}|^2,
       \label{eq:ring-polymer-potential}\\
  \omega_L&=\frac{L}{\beta\hbar},\qquad
  \bm R_i^{(L+1)}=\bm R_i^{(1)}.
\end{align}
Each nucleus becomes a closed ring of $L$ beads connected by springs; the
physical potential acts within each slice. Beads resolve imaginary time,
not a real trajectory. Periodic paths need consistent images and, where
relevant, winding sectors. The equivalent convention samples
$L U_L^{\mathrm{eff}}$ at $LT$. At $L=1$ the classical configuration
weight is recovered; the symmetric primitive error is $\mathcal O(L^{-2})$
for smooth potentials \cite{tuckerman2023,ceriotti2010pimd}.

\paragraph{Sampling and observables}

Auxiliary momenta sample
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal H_{\mathrm{samp}}
   =\sum_{i,j}\frac{|\bm\pi_i^{(j)}|^2}{2\mu_i^{(j)}}
    +U_L^{\mathrm{eff}}.
\end{equation}
Fictitious masses $\mu$ affect efficiency; physical masses $m$ in the
springs determine quantum delocalization. Normal-mode thermostats such as
PILE handle stiff springs \cite{ceriotti2010pimd}. PIMD simulation time is
only a sampling variable; quantum transport or rates require additional
real-time approximations such as RPMD/CMD.

For position-diagonal observables,
\begin{equation}
  \langle A\rangle_{\mathrm q}
  =\lim_{L\to\infty}\left\langle
      \frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}A(\bm R^{(j)})\right\rangle_L,
\end{equation}
A nonlinear observable at the centroid generally differs from the bead
average. A centroid-virial physical energy estimator is
\begin{align}
  \overline{\bm R}_i&=\frac{1}{L}\sum_j\bm R_i^{(j)},\\
  K_{\mathrm{cv}}&=\frac{3N}{2\beta}
      -\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{i,j}
       (\bm R_i^{(j)}-\overline{\bm R}_i)\cdot\bm F_i^{(j)},\\
  E_{\mathrm{est}}&=K_{\mathrm{cv}}
       +\frac{1}{L}\sum_j U(\bm R^{(j)};\bm h),
       \label{eq:pimd-energy-estimator}
\end{align}
Use physical forces and unwrapped paths, with matching modifications for
constraints or removed degrees of freedom \cite{yamamoto2005}.
NPT also requires a shared cell, quantum pressure estimator, and correct
cell measure \cite{ceriotti2014ipi}:
\begin{align}
  \langle H\rangle_{\mathrm q}
    &=\lim_{L\to\infty}\langle E_{\mathrm{est}}+PV\rangle_L,\\
  C_P^{\mathrm q}
    &=\left(\frac{\partial\langle H\rangle_{\mathrm q}}{\partial T}
      \right)_{N,P}.
      \label{eq:pimd-cp}
\end{align}
Exclude fictitious kinetic and spring energies from physical enthalpy.
A mean-energy estimator's variance is not automatically the quantum
enthalpy variance; direct heat-capacity estimators must differentiate the
action, normalization, and estimator \cite{yamamoto2005}.

\paragraph{Prospective validation}

Converge beads at every derivative temperature, starting with
$\beta\hbar\omega_{\max}/L\ll1$. Each step costs approximately $L$ force
evaluations. An i-PI/PET interface \cite{ipi-documentation} should first
recover $L=1$ classical results, then stable quantum harmonic benchmarks,
before loaded-MOF NPT tests. Converge timestep, thermostats, cell size, and
sampling as well as beads; validate quantum-displaced configurations against
the potential's domain. For $\mathrm{H_2O}$ loadings, test O--H vibrations
and hydrogen-bond observables alongside heat capacity; high-frequency
hydrogen modes can require more beads even near room temperature.
Converged PIMD would test the hybrid's missing quantum
anharmonic and pressure--volume terms. Do not add a harmonic quantum
correction again to PIMD results.

\subsection{Graph Theory}
\label{sec:graph-theory}

Graphs describe atomic neighborhoods and derivative dependencies
\cite{diestel2025,bronstein2021}. Their roles are distinguished in
Table~\ref{tab:graph-roles}.

\subsubsection{Adjacency, paths, and receptive fields}

A graph is described by $\mathcal G=(\mathcal V,\mathcal E)$, where $\mathcal V$ is the set of vertices and $\mathcal E$ is the set of edges.
For instance, suppose we have a set of nodes represented by indices $\mathcal V=\{1,2,3,4\}$,
the edges correspond to the connections between these nodes, we can represent them as a set of pairs $\mathcal E=\{\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{3,4\}\}$.
In the case where there is no directionality in the connections,
the graph is said to be undirected and each edge is represented as an unordered pair of vertices (a set).
If the connections have a direction, the graph is said to be directed and each edge is represented as an ordered pair of vertices (a tuple).
We can easily represent the edges between nodes by constructing the adjacency matrix $\bm A$,
where $A_{ij}=1$ if there is an edge between nodes $i$ and $j$, and $A_{ij}=0$ otherwise.
The neighborhood of a node $i$, denoted as $\mathcal N_i$, is the set of nodes that are directly connected to $i$ by an edge,
which can be defined as $\mathcal N_i=\{j:A_{ij}=1\}$.
Atomic graphs are directed periodic multigraphs: messages have directions,
and repeated images can connect the same atom labels. Edges indicate
interactions, not necessarily chemical bonds. For a simple undirected graph,

\begin{equation}
  A_{ij}=\begin{cases}
    1,&\{i,j\}\in\mathcal E,\\
    0,&\text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \qquad \mathcal N_i=\{j:A_{ij}=1\}.
\end{equation}

The degree of a node is the number of nodes connected to it. For a node $i$, the degree is $d_i=|\mathcal N_i|$,
which allows us to define the degree matrix $\bm D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_{|\mathcal V|})$.
Graph distance counts edges in a shortest path. Its $k$-hop ball is

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal B_k(i)=\{j:d_{\mathcal G}(i,j)\leq k\}.
\end{equation}

For $1-2-3-4$, $\mathcal B_1(1)=\{1,2\}$ and
$\mathcal B_2(1)=\{1,2,3\}$. Boolean powers of adjacency record
reachability (Eq.~\eqref{eq:hop-pattern}). With $L$ local message-passing
layers, a node depends on its $L$-hop neighborhood. Atoms influencing the
same nonlinear readout can have mixed second derivatives beyond one hop.

Later on, we will apply functions on the graph representation.
When using nodes as atoms and edges as interactions, it is important that the ajecency matrix stays invariant with respect to node permuations.
For instance, when trying to compute the energy using the graph representation, the result should not depend on the order of the nodes.
So $f(\bm A)=f(\bm P\bm A\bm P^{\mathrm T})$, where $\bm P$ is a permutation matrix.
Node outputs must permute consistently; total energy must remain invariant
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:graph-permutation}). The computation graph instead records
operations and directed dependencies. Forward evaluation/AD follows its
acyclic order; reverse AD applies the chain rule backward \cite{baydin2018}.

\subsubsection{Graph coloring and sparse automatic differentiation}
\label{sec:graph-coloring}

A proper vertex coloring assigns an integer $c(q)\in\{1,\ldots,n_c\}$
to each vertex so that adjacent vertices have different colors.
For differentiation, a color identifies input directions that can be
processed together. The relevant edges must therefore represent conflicts
in derivative recovery, rather than simply atomic neighbor relations.

ASD separates structural preparation from numerical differentiation
\cite{hill2025asd}:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\text{detect pattern}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{color}}
    _{\text{preparation}}
  \ \longrightarrow\
  \underbrace{\text{evaluate colored products}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{decompress}}
    _{\text{numerical evaluation}}.
  \label{eq:asd-preparation-pipeline}
\end{equation}
Preparation can be reused while the pattern remains valid.

\paragraph{From derivatives to a conflict graph.}
For a function $\bm f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$, let
$J_{pq}=\partial f_p/\partial x_q$. A structural sparsity pattern
$\bm S\in\{0,1\}^{m\times n}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  S_{pq}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ J_{pq}=0,
  \qquad \mathcal R_q=\{p:S_{pq}=1\}.
\end{equation}
Thus $\mathcal R_q$ lists the output rows that column $q$ may affect.
A numerical zero at one configuration does not necessarily imply a
structural zero. Two columns conflict when their supports overlap, so the
\emph{column-intersection graph} has one vertex per input coordinate and
edges
\begin{equation}
  \{q,r\}\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{col}}
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    q\ne r\ \text{and}\ \mathcal R_q\cap\mathcal R_r\ne\varnothing.
\end{equation}
Proper coloring of this graph ensures that columns of the same color
have disjoint supports, also called structural orthogonality
\cite{hill2025asd}.

The pattern can be inferred by propagating input-dependency sets through
the computation graph, without evaluating every derivative. For addition,
for example, the dependency set of $z=u+v$ is the union of those of $u$
and $v$. A global pattern covers the input domain; a local pattern may
exploit the active branches at one input but requires revalidation
\cite{hill2025asd}.

\paragraph{Colored seeds, compression, and recovery.}
Forward AD evaluates a Jacobian-vector product without first forming
$\bm J$. The ordinary seed $\bm e_q$ yields column $q$; using all $n$
coordinate seeds would therefore require $n$ directional evaluations.
For a composition $\bm f=\bm h\circ\bm g$, the chain rule acts on a seed as
\begin{equation}
  \bm J_f(\bm x)\bm v
    =\bm J_h(\bm g(\bm x))\bigl[\bm J_g(\bm x)\bm v\bigr].
  \label{eq:matrix-free-chain-rule}
\end{equation}
AD propagates the direction through these operations without storing the
intermediate Jacobian matrices \cite{baydin2018}.
Construct one seed per color:
\begin{equation}
  \bm v_a=\sum_{q:c(q)=a}\bm e_q,
  \qquad V_{qa}=\begin{cases}1,&c(q)=a,\\0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
  \qquad \bm Y=\bm J\bm V.
  \label{eq:graph-colored-seeds}
\end{equation}
Each compressed entry is a sum,
$Y_{pa}=\sum_{q:c(q)=a}J_{pq}$. Structural orthogonality guarantees that
at most one term is permitted to be nonzero in a given row. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  J_{pq}=Y_{p,c(q)}\quad\text{for }S_{pq}=1.
  \label{eq:graph-jacobian-recovery}
\end{equation}
The pattern identifies which column supplied each value. Sparse automatic
differentiation (ASD) combines this graph-based preparation with ordinary
AD and subsequent decompression; the AD still differentiates the original
function. The number of directions falls from $n$ to $n_c$, rather than
computing a dense derivative and discarding its zeros afterward.

Figure~\ref{fig:asd-compression-recovery} illustrates both numerical steps.

Then $\bm J_f=\bm H$, and a forward derivative of the reverse-mode
energy gradient gives $\bm H\bm v_a$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian-vector-product}).
Coloring therefore groups Cartesian coordinates whose Hessian columns
can be recovered from the same Hessian-vector product.

\paragraph{A worked example.}
Consider the symmetric tridiagonal Hessian
\begin{equation}
  \bm M=\begin{pmatrix}
    a&b&0&0\\ b&c&d&0\\ 0&d&e&f\\ 0&0&f&g
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:graph-coloring-example}
\end{equation}
Its off-diagonal sparsity graph is the chain $1-2-3-4$.
Coloring that chain with $(1,2,1,2)$ is proper, but does not permit direct
column recovery: columns 1 and 3 both affect row 2, yielding the
inseparable sum $b+d$. The column-intersection graph also connects
vertices at distance two along the chain. A valid coloring of this larger
conflict graph is $(1,2,3,1)$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \bm V=\begin{pmatrix}
    1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \bm M\bm V=\begin{pmatrix}
    a&b&0\\b&c&d\\f&d&e\\g&0&f
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:graph-direct-compression}
\end{equation}
The first product combines columns 1 and 4, whose supports are disjoint:
rows 1--2 supply column 1 and rows 3--4 supply column 4. Three products
recover every entry, whereas dense recovery would use four. For a longer
tridiagonal matrix, repeating three colors still suffices, so the benefit
grows with matrix size.

\paragraph{Star coloring exploits Hessian symmetry.}
For a symmetric Hessian, $H_{pq}=H_{qp}$ supplies two possible compressed
locations for each off-diagonal value: $Y_{p,c(q)}$ and $Y_{q,c(p)}$.
It is sufficient that one of these is uncontaminated. This permits a less
restrictive coloring than requiring every pair of column supports to be
disjoint \cite{gebremedhin2009hessian}.

A \emph{star coloring} is a proper coloring of the off-diagonal Hessian
sparsity graph in which every path on four distinct vertices uses at least
three colors. Equivalently, the subgraph formed by any two colors is a
collection of stars. In each star, the center is the hub and its neighbors
are spokes. A spoke has only one neighbor with the hub's color, so the
hub--spoke entry can be extracted from that spoke's row; symmetry supplies
the transposed entry. Proper coloring also keeps diagonal entries separate
from off-diagonal contributions \cite{asdexcoloring}.

For the same chain, $(1,2,1,3)$ is a star coloring. Its compression is
\begin{equation}
  \bm W=\begin{pmatrix}
    1&0&0\\0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \bm B=\bm M\bm W=\begin{pmatrix}
    a&b&0\\b+d&c&0\\e&d&f\\f&0&g
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:graph-star-compression}
\end{equation}
Although $B_{21}=b+d$ cannot be separated directly, $b=B_{12}$ and
$d=B_{32}$ are individually available from the opposite orientations.
The remaining values are $a=B_{11}$, $c=B_{22}$, $e=B_{31}$,
$f=B_{33}=B_{41}$, and $g=B_{43}$.
This example uses three colors with either strategy; star coloring can
reduce the count further for other symmetric sparsity patterns.

\paragraph{Connection to the project implementation.}
SADMOF derives an atom-pair pattern from PET's neighborhood graph and the
chosen hop reach, then represents each permitted atom pair by a Cartesian
$3\times3$ block. The project passes this pattern to ASDEX for symmetric
Hessian coloring and uses forward-over-reverse AD for the colored products
(Section~\ref{sec:asd-method}). Atom-block indices describe sparsity; the
differentiated variables remain the $3N$ Cartesian coordinates.
The recovered Hessian is subsequently mass weighted and diagonalized to
obtain the harmonic frequencies.

The pattern must include every actual coupling for exact recovery.
For example, if the true matrix above also had
$M_{24}=M_{42}=\delta$ but the pattern omitted this pair, the direct
coloring would still combine columns 1 and 4 and incorrectly recover
$M_{21}$ as $b+\delta$. Thus an underestimated pattern can corrupt retained
entries as well as omit missing ones. Choosing fewer hops changes the
assumed sparsity and requires convergence checks. By contrast,
\texttt{chunk\_size} only controls how many colored directions are
evaluated together. A greedy algorithm processes vertices in an ordering and assigns each
the smallest admissible color; ordering affects the resulting count.
For star coloring, admissibility also enforces the four-vertex-path rule.
Greedy coloring need not find the minimum possible number of colors to
give correct recovery; extra colors cost work \cite{asdexcoloring}.

\subsection{Machine-learned interatomic potentials}
\label{sec:mlip-theory}

The potential is what drives the molecular dynamics simulation.
However, using first-principles methods like DFT to compute the potential energy surface would be very expensive.
On the other hand, using simple potentials like Lennard-Jones or Morse potentials would be too inaccurate for complex systems like MOFs.
Machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) provide a way to approximate the potential energy surface 
by training neural networks on DFT reference data.
This methods represents a good trade-off between accuracy and computational cost, provided it was trained on a representative dataset.
It may not be able to give good results for configurations that are far from the training data.
This is why there will be an important section about uncertainty quantification in one of the following chapters.

\subsubsection{Energy, forces, and atomic graphs}

An MLIP approximates the Born--Oppenheimer map
$(\bm R,\bm h,\bm Z)\mapsto U$ with fitted parameters $\theta$.
A local, extensive decomposition is

\begin{equation}
  U_\theta(\bm R,\bm h,\bm Z)
  = U_{\mathrm{base}}(\bm Z)
  + \sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_i(\mathcal N_i;\theta),
  \label{eq:mlip-energy}
\end{equation}

The optional baseline depends only on composition. Atomic energies are model
contributions, not unique observables. At bounded neighbor count, evaluation
cost scales approximately linearly with atom count. Differentiating the
same scalar energy gives conservative forces and a Cartesian Hessian:

\begin{equation}
  \bm F_i=-\frac{\partial U_\theta}{\partial \bm R_i},
  \label{eq:mlip-force}
\end{equation}

Atoms are graph vertices with species features. Periodic directed edges
$(v,u,\bm n)$ carry relative geometry:

\begin{align}
  \bm r_{uv\bm n}
    &= \bm R_v+\bm h\bm n-\bm R_u,
  \\
  r_{uv\bm n}&=\lVert\bm r_{uv\bm n}\rVert < r_{\mathrm c},
  \label{eq:periodic-edge}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal N_u
  =\{(v,\bm n):(v,u,\bm n)\in\mathcal E\}.
  \label{eq:periodic-neighborhood}
\end{equation}

Retaining image labels distinguishes repeated neighbors. Species embeddings
and radial edge features provide numerical inputs:

\begin{equation}
  \bm x_u^{(0)}=\bm E_Z[Z_u]\in\mathbb R^d,
  \label{eq:species-embedding}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \bm x_{uv\bm n}^{\mathrm{edge}}
  =f_{\mathrm c}(r_{uv\bm n})
   \bigl[\varphi_1(r_{uv\bm n}),\ldots,
          \varphi_K(r_{uv\bm n})\bigr],
  \label{eq:radial-embedding}
\end{equation}

Directional models also use displacement vectors or their learned functions;
latent feature vectors need not transform as physical vectors.

Figures~\ref{fig:cutoff-single} and \ref{fig:cutoff-functions} illustrate
cutoff weighting. Smooth forces and Hessians require consistent derivatives
through attention normalization and readout as well as radial features.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/cutoff_single.pdf}
  \caption{A smooth radial cutoff suppresses a neighbor contribution as its
  distance approaches the outer cutoff $r_{\mathrm c}$. Smooth decay is needed
  for continuous energies, forces, and Hessians. Figure from the PET paper
  \cite{pet}.}
  \label{fig:cutoff-single}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/cutoff_functions.pdf}
  \caption{Comparison of radial cutoff functions used to attenuate neighbor
  contributions near $r_{\mathrm c}$. The value and the derivatives relevant
  to the model must be treated consistently in every energy contribution.
  Figure from the PET paper \cite{pet}.}
  \label{fig:cutoff-functions}
\end{figure}

Node-wise $F$ and scalar readout $f$ obey
\begin{align}
  F(\bm P\bm X,\bm P\bm A\bm P^{\mathrm T})
    &=\bm P F(\bm X,\bm A),
  \\
  f(\bm P\bm X,\bm P\bm A\bm P^{\mathrm T})
    &=f(\bm X,\bm A).
  \label{eq:graph-permutation}
\end{align}
Relabel edge attributes and periodic images consistently.

\subsubsection{Message passing}

Each layer combines incoming messages with a symmetric aggregation
$\bigoplus$ (usually a sum), then updates the node representation
\cite{bronstein2021}:
\begin{equation}
  \bm h_u
  =\phi\!\left(
      \bm x_u,
      \bigoplus_{(v,\bm n)\in\mathcal N_u}
      \psi\!\left(\bm x_u,\bm x_v,
                   \bm x_{uv\bm n}^{\mathrm{edge}}\right)
    \right).
  \label{eq:message-passing}
\end{equation}
Repeating the update through $L$ layers creates many-body features over an
$L$-hop receptive field. A shared readout
$\varepsilon_u=\rho(\bm h_u^{(L)})$ gives the total energy through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mlip-energy}.

\subsubsection{Point Edge Transformer architecture}
\label{sec:pet-architecture}

PET retains distinct directed-edge messages while attention mixes all tokens
in one atomic environment (Figure~\ref{fig:pet-architecture}) \cite{pet}.
It underlies PET-MAD \cite{petmad}. In the original formulation,
\begin{align}
  \bm p_{ij}^{(\ell)}
    &=\operatorname{SiLU}\bigl(\bm W_r^{(\ell)}\bm r_{ij}
                                      +\bm b_r^{(\ell)}\bigr),
  \\
  \bm t_{ij}^{(\ell)}
    &=\mathrm{MLP}_\ell\!\left(
      \bm m_{j\to i}^{(\ell-1)}\Vert
      \bm E_n^{(\ell)}[Z_j]\Vert
      \bm p_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right),
  \label{eq:pet-token}
\end{align}

Here $\Vert$ concatenates the incoming message, neighbor embedding, and
position encoding. The MLP maps $3d_{\mathrm{PET}}$ features to
$d_{\mathrm{PET}}$; the first block omits the incoming message. A separate
central token is $\bm t_{i0}^{(\ell)}=\bm E_c^{(\ell)}[Z_i]$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/pet_final.pdf}
  \caption{Point Edge Transformer (PET) message-passing architecture. Edge
  messages associated with a central atom are jointly processed by attention
  while retaining their individual edge identities for the outgoing update.
  Figure from the PET paper \cite{pet}.}
  \label{fig:pet-architecture}
\end{figure}

For one attention head,

\begin{align}
  \bm q_a&=\bm W_Q\bm t_a,
  &\bm k_b&=\bm W_K\bm t_b,
  \\
  \bm v_b&=\bm W_V\bm t_b,
  \\
  \alpha_{ab}
    &=\frac{\exp(\bm q_a^{\mathrm T}\bm k_b/\sqrt{d_k})
                    c_b}
            {\sum_c\exp(\bm q_a^{\mathrm T}\bm k_c/\sqrt{d_k})
                    c_c},
  \\
  \bm y_a&=\sum_b\alpha_{ab}\bm v_b.
  \label{eq:self-attention}
\end{align}

With $c_0=1$ and $c_j=f_{\mathrm c}(r_{ij})$, departing neighbors vanish
from both numerator and normalization. Multi-head attention, feed-forward
layers, and residual connections preserve neighbor permutation equivariance.
Attention layers enrich interactions within one environment; message-passing
blocks extend the receptive field. Local attention cost is quadratic in
neighbor count, but total cost remains approximately linear in atom count
at fixed architecture and bounded neighborhoods.

Outgoing edge tokens update the messages. The original summed-edge readout is

\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  U_\theta={}&\sum_i b_{Z_i}
  +\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_i\Bigl[
      H_c^{(\ell)}(\bm x_{i0}^{(\ell)})
  \\[-0.2em]
  &+\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}f_{\mathrm c}(r_{ij})
                 H_n^{(\ell)}(\bm x_{ij}^{(\ell)})\Bigr],
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:pet-readout}
\end{equation}

$H_c$ and $H_n$ are learned heads, $\bm x$ are output tokens, and $b_Z$
are species baselines. Every block contributes; cutoff weighting also enters
the readout. Edge contributions are learned many-body terms, not pair energies.
Project-specific dimensions appear in Section~\ref{sec:pet-implementation}.

\subsubsection{Physical symmetries and ECSE}

Scalar energies should respect translation, rotation/reflection, and
identical-atom permutation:

\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  U(\bm R)&=U(\bm R+\bm a)=U(\bm Q\bm R)
  \\
           &=U(\bm P\bm R),\qquad \bm Q\in O(3).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:energy-symmetry}
\end{equation}

Forces then transform equivariantly,
$\bm F_i(\bm Q\bm R)=\bm Q\bm F_i(\bm R)$; periodic tests rotate both
positions and cell. PET enforces translation and permutation symmetries,
but learns rotational behavior through augmentation. Reported rotational
errors below prediction errors \cite{petmad} do not validate this checkpoint
on loaded MOF-5.

The optional Equivariant Coordinate System Ensemble (ECSE) imposes exact
rotation symmetry through local frames from ordered noncollinear neighbor
pairs \cite{pet}:

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ecse_final.pdf}
  \caption{Equivariant Coordinate System Ensemble (ECSE). Local coordinate
  frames constructed from noncollinear neighbor pairs rotate with the atomic
  environment; predictions evaluated in these frames are transformed as
  needed and smoothly averaged. Figure from the PET paper \cite{pet}.}
  \label{fig:ecse}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_i^{\mathrm{ECSE}}=
    \frac{\sum_{j\ne k}w_{ijk}\,
          \varepsilon_i^0(\bm Q_{ijk}^{\mathrm T}\mathcal N_i)}
         {\sum_{j\ne k}w_{ijk}},
  \label{eq:ecse}
\end{equation}

$\bm Q_{ijk}^{\mathrm T}\mathcal N_i$ expresses displacements in a
co-rotating frame; vector/tensor outputs are transformed back before averaging.
These proper $SO(3)$ rotations alone do not enforce reflection invariance.
Smooth radial/angular weights suppress cutoff and nearly collinear frames;
fully collinear environments need a fallback. The full frame and weight
dependence must be differentiated for forces and Hessians. Tight frame
selection can amplify derivative errors, and the proof-of-principle overhead
is roughly $10^3$ \cite{pet}. ECSE is not enabled here.

\subsubsection{Training and validity}

A generic energy--force objective is

\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathcal L(\theta)=\sum_s\Biggl[{}
    w_E\left(\frac{U_\theta^{(s)}-U_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(s)}}{N_s}\right)^2
  \\
    +\frac{w_F}{3N_s}\sum_{i\alpha}
      \left(F_{i\alpha,\theta}^{(s)}
                 -F_{i\alpha,\mathrm{ref}}^{(s)}\right)^2
  \Biggr],
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:mlip-loss}
\end{equation}

Stress terms and regularization may be added. PET's original loss uses a
different normalization with moving validation errors, species baselines,
Adam, and rotational augmentation \cite{pet}. PET-MAD is pretrained on a
chemically diverse DFT dataset \cite{petmad}.

An MLIP inherits its reference method's approximations and may extrapolate
poorly to unfamiliar environments. Hold-out errors, trajectory stability,
and uncertainty indicators test different aspects of validity. Curvatures
need explicit validation because the usual loss does not constrain Hessians
directly; PET's methane benchmarks do not establish accuracy for adsorption
in MOF-5.

\subsubsection{LLPR model uncertainty and CEA propagation}
\label{sec:llpr-cea}

Finite-trajectory sampling error is distinct from potential uncertainty.
LLPR varies the final readout of a fixed PET representation \cite{bigi2024}.
For central energy $\bar U=\bm w_0^{\mathrm T}\bm f(\bm x)$ and
covariance-set features $\bm F$,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \sigma_{\mathrm{LLPR}}^2(\bm x)
  ={}&\alpha^2\bm f(\bm x)^{\mathrm T}
  \\
  &\times\left(\bm F^{\mathrm T}\bm F
        +\lambda\bm I\right)^{-1}\bm f(\bm x).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:llpr-variance}
\end{equation}
The regularizer $\lambda$ stabilizes weakly constrained directions; $\alpha$
is calibrated against energy residuals on a disjoint reference-labeled set
using the base potential's electronic-structure definition. Predictions
cannot calibrate their own error.

The exported \texttt{energy\_\allowbreak{}uncertainty} is an energy standard deviation.
Property propagation instead needs persistent, signed energy variations:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \bm w_i&\sim\mathcal N\!\left[
    \bm w_0,
    \alpha^2\left(\bm F^{\mathrm T}\bm F
                         +\lambda\bm I\right)^{-1}\right],
  \\
  U^{(i)}(\bm x)&=\bm w_i^{\mathrm T}\bm f(\bm x),
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:llpr-members}
\end{equation}
The exported \texttt{energy\_\allowbreak{}ensemble} preserves member identity across
configurations. Its large-ensemble spread approaches the analytical LLPR
value, but cannot capture bias shared by the frozen representation.

Following Imbalzano \emph{et al.}\ \cite{imbalzano2021}, central-trajectory
frames supply
\begin{align}
  \Delta U_t^{(i)} &= U_t^{(i)}-\bar U_t,\\
  H_t^{(i)} &= K_t+U_t^{(i)}+PV_t,
  \label{eq:llpr-frame-enthalpy}
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
  \left\langle H^{(i)}\right\rangle_i^{\mathrm{direct}}
  =\frac{\sum_t e^{-\beta\Delta U_t^{(i)}}H_t^{(i)}}
  {\sum_t e^{-\beta\Delta U_t^{(i)}}},
  \label{eq:llpr-direct}
\end{equation}
Extensive energy differences can collapse the exponential weights. The
primary large-system estimate uses first-order cumulant expansion (CEA):
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \left\langle H^{(i)}\right\rangle_i^{\mathrm{CEA}}
  ={}&\left\langle H^{(i)}\right\rangle_{\bar U}
  \\
  &-\beta\operatorname{cov}_{\bar U}
  \left(H^{(i)},\Delta U^{(i)}\right),
  \\
  &\beta=(k_{\mathrm B}T)^{-1}.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:llpr-cea}
\end{equation}
Direct reweighting remains a diagnostic:
$N_{\mathrm{eff}}=1/\sum_t\widetilde w_t^2$ should remain adequate, and
$\operatorname{var}_t(\beta\Delta U_t^{(i)})\ll1$ is needed for controlled
first-order CEA.

Average replicas member by member, preserve indices across temperatures,
and differentiate each complete enthalpy curve:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  C_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{(i)}(T)
  &=\frac{\mathrm d\langle H^{(i)}\rangle_i^{\mathrm{CEA}}}
          {\mathrm dT},
  \\
  \sigma_{\mathrm{LLPR},C_P}(T)
  &=\left\{\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}
  \left[C_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{(i)}(T)
  -\overline C_{P,\mathrm{cl}}(T)\right]^2\right\}^{1/2}.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:llpr-cp-spread}
\end{equation}
The final property spread is a predictive standard deviation; do not divide
it by $\sqrt M$.

\subsubsection{Gaussian NPT propagation}
\label{sec:gaussian-propagation}

Direct propagation of shallow ensembles (DPOSE) combines member properties
before measuring their spread \cite{kellner2024}. The saved comparison uses
a Gaussian NPT approximation on central-model trajectories. Define
$Q_i=U_i+PV$ and $\Delta U_i=U_i-U_0$.
Joint Gaussianity leaves covariance unchanged under exponential reweighting,
giving
\begin{equation}
 C_{P,i}^{\mathrm G}\simeq\frac{f}{2}k_{\mathrm B}
       +\frac{\operatorname{var}_0(Q_i)}{k_{\mathrm B}T^2}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-member-cp}
\end{equation}
Here $f=3N-3$. The central-model fluctuation estimator is exact under the
usual equilibrium assumptions; using central-trajectory variances for other
members needs Gaussian closure. Pair member indices with harmonic corrections.

The main Gaussian plots replace only the propagated model spread around the
central enthalpy-derivative curve. The separate variance-based hybrid also
replaces the central classical value and estimates sampling error from
squared configurational-enthalpy fluctuations. Neither guarantees smaller
or better calibrated uncertainty.

\subsubsection{Classical versus quantum vibrations}

Classical harmonic modes contribute $k_{\mathrm B}$ each at every temperature.
Quantum occupation decreases when $\hbar\omega\geq k_{\mathrm B}T$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:quantum-harmonic-cv}). Longer classical trajectories cannot
recover that suppression.

\section{Reference evidence and the hybrid approximation}

Kapil \emph{et al.}\ tested empty MOF-5 and 50-, 100-, and 150-methane cells
\cite{kapil2019}. Their hybrid strategy motivates this project; its accuracy
on PET remains to be established.

\subsection{Differences from the reference calculation}
\label{sec:reference-comparison}

The reference used QuickFF \cite{quickff}: B3LYP cluster-based bonded terms,
MBIS charges from periodic PBE, Gaussian electrostatics, and MM3 Buckingham
interactions with a \SI{15}{\angstrom} cutoff and tail corrections.
RASPA insertion and canonical Monte Carlo preceded MD. Here PET supplies all
energies, forces, and Hessians after independent random guest placement.

Both workflows differentiate neighboring equilibrium enthalpies:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  C_P(T)&\simeq
  \frac{\langle H\rangle_{T+\Delta T}-\langle H\rangle_{T-\Delta T}}
       {2\Delta T},
  \\
  \Delta T&=\SI{25}{K},
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:finite-difference-cp}
\end{equation}
The reference used optimized Yaff Hessians and TAMkin modes, compared QuickFF
and UFF4MOF for the empty host, and optimized five Monte Carlo configurations
for the 100-methane harmonic calculation.

\subsection{Physical conclusions}
\label{sec:reference-conclusions}

The reference found nearly harmonic, quantum-mechanical empty MOF-5
(roughly \SI{0.7}{J\per g\per K} near room temperature). Classical
occupations overestimate its heat capacity. Methane introduces anharmonic
host--guest motion and a loading-dependent minimum near \SI{200}{K},
associated with changing attractive interactions as guests become mobile.
The combination
\begin{equation}
          C \approx C_{\mathrm{qn}}^{\mathrm{har}}
          - C_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{har}}
          + C_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{anh}}
          \label{eq:quantum-correction}
        \end{equation}
reproduced the minimum qualitatively and was useful above about \SI{200}{K}
in their 100-methane test. A loaded-system spectrum is essential: the empty
Hessian omits guest modes and host--guest coupling. Equation~\eqref{eq:hybrid-cp}
gives the operational PET estimator.

\subsection{Harmonic approximation and Cartesian Hessian}

Let $\bm R_0$ be a stationary reference structure and
$\bm u=\bm R-\bm R_0$. Expanding the potential energy gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  U(\bm R_0+\bm u)={}&U(\bm R_0)
  +\underbrace{\nabla U(\bm R_0)\cdot\bm u}
       _{=0\ \mathrm{at\ a\ minimum}}
  \\
  &+\frac{1}{2}\bm u^{\mathrm T}\bm H\bm u
  +\mathcal O(u^3),
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:taylor}
\end{equation}
with Cartesian Hessian
\begin{equation}
  H_{i\alpha,j\beta}
  =\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial R_{i\alpha}\partial R_{j\beta}}
  =-\frac{\partial F_{i\alpha}}{\partial R_{j\beta}}.
  \label{eq:hessian}
\end{equation}
For $N$ atoms, $\bm H$ has dimensions $3N\times3N$; for the 100-methane cell
this is $2772\times2772$.

\subsubsection{Automatic differentiation}

At fixed parameters, a neural-network energy composes differentiable operations:
\begin{equation}
  \bm z^{(0)}=\bm R,\qquad
  \bm z^{(\ell+1)}=\phi_\ell(\bm z^{(\ell)};\theta),\qquad
  U_\theta=\rho(\bm z^{(L)}).
  \label{eq:nn-computational-graph}
\end{equation}
AD applies their chain rules without a finite-displacement step
\cite{baydin2018}. It differentiates the implemented potential, up to
floating-point error; it does not improve the potential's accuracy.
For $\bm f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$,
\begin{equation}
  (\bm J_f)_{pq}=\frac{\partial f_p}{\partial x_q},
  \qquad \bm J_f\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}.
  \label{eq:jacobian-definition}
\end{equation}
Forward AD evaluates $\bm J_f\bm v$ (JVP); reverse AD evaluates
$\bm w^{\mathrm T}\bm J_f$ (VJP). Dense recovery requires $n$ forward
or $m$ reverse seeds, without storing layer Jacobians \cite{hill2025asd}.
A single reverse sweep yields $\nabla U$ and hence all forces.
Forward-over-reverse evaluates
\begin{equation}
  \bm H\bm v=
  \left.\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\epsilon}
  \nabla_{\bm R}U_\theta(\bm R+\epsilon\bm v)
  \right|_{\epsilon=0}.
  \label{eq:hessian-vector-product}
\end{equation}
Dense Hessian recovery needs $3N$ such products. Sparse reconstruction reduces
the seed count; cutoff smoothness and neighbor-selection conventions still
control which function is differentiated.

\subsubsection{Sparse Hessian reconstruction with SADMOF}
\label{sec:asd-method}

SADMOF constructs an atom-pair pattern from PET's selected-neighbor graph,
then expands each pair into a Cartesian $3\times3$ block \cite{sadMof}.
For $L$ message-passing layers, the maximum Hessian reach is $K=2L$ for
node readout and $K=2L+1$ for PET edge readout. The paper's MACE-MP-0 medium,
PET-XS, and PET-S reaches are four, five, and seven hops. Boolean adjacency gives
\begin{equation}
  \bm P^{(k)}=\bm I\lor\bm A\lor\bm A^2\lor\cdots\lor\bm A^k,
  \label{eq:hop-pattern}
\end{equation}
At $k=K$ the pattern contains all couplings under the chosen derivative
convention; $k<K$ truncates it. Additional nonlocal terms need separate analysis.
Structural sparsity requires
\begin{equation}
  S_{pq}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H_{pq}=0
  \label{eq:hessian-sparsity-pattern}
\end{equation}
An overestimate costs entries and colors. An underestimate can both discard
curvature and contaminate retained entries. Mixed second derivatives depend
on nonlinear readout, not just direct interaction edges.

As developed in Section~\ref{sec:graph-coloring}, direct column recovery
connects columns with overlapping supports:
\begin{equation}
  (q,r)\in\mathcal E_S
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \exists p:\ S_{pq}=S_{pr}=1.
  \label{eq:column-intersection-graph}
\end{equation}
A proper coloring makes same-color columns structurally orthogonal.
Greedy heuristics need not minimize color count to recover exact entries
\cite{hill2025asd}. Define one seed per color class,
\begin{equation}
  \bm v_a=\sum_{q\in\mathcal C_a}\bm e_q,
  \qquad
  \bm H\bm v_a=\sum_{q\in\mathcal C_a}\bm H\bm e_q.
  \label{eq:colored-hvp}
\end{equation}
and collect seeds in $\bm V\in\{0,1\}^{3N\times n_c}$:
\begin{equation}
  \bm Y=\bm H\bm V,
  \qquad
  Y_{pa}=\sum_{q:c(q)=a}H_{pq}.
  \label{eq:compressed-hessian}
\end{equation}
Disjoint supports permit
\begin{equation}
  H_{pq}=Y_{p,c(q)}\qquad\text{when }S_{pq}=1,
  \label{eq:hessian-decompression}
\end{equation}
with zeros outside the pattern.

ASDEX instead star-colors the symmetric Hessian graph. For each pair
$\{p,q\}$, a hub--spoke orientation selects an uncontaminated compressed
location and writes it to both $H_{pq}$ and $H_{qp}$. This can reduce colors
\cite{hill2025asd,sadMof}. Atom-block coloring can be lifted to coordinate
colors $3c_i+d$, $d\in\{0,1,2\}$; availability depends on the installed
ASDEX implementation.

The pipeline is summarized by
\begin{equation}
  \bm S\ \xrightarrow{\mathrm{color}}\ \bm V
  \ \xrightarrow{\mathrm{HVPs}}\ \bm Y=\bm H\bm V
  \ \xrightarrow{\mathrm{recover}}\ \bm H,
  \qquad \text{ideal AD reduction}=\frac{3N}{n_c}.
\end{equation}
Pattern setup, compilation, decompression, and GPU utilization limit the
wall-time gain. Reuse a coloring only while its structural pattern remains valid.

The wrapper uses \texttt{atoms\_\allowbreak{}to\_\allowbreak{}inputs}, \texttt{sparsity\_\allowbreak{}pattern},
\texttt{asdex.\allowbreak{}hessian\_\allowbreak{}coloring\_\allowbreak{}from\_\allowbreak{}sparsity},
\texttt{get\_\allowbreak{}energy\_\allowbreak{}fn}, \texttt{get\_\allowbreak{}hessian\_\allowbreak{}fn}, and \texttt{jax.\allowbreak{}jit}.
It densifies the sparse result and removes padding before eigendecomposition;
only the AD stage exploits sparsity.

\texttt{no\_\allowbreak{}shadow=True} stops adaptive-neighbor-selection derivatives and
uses the selected graph. Shadow derivatives introduce extra couplings;
\texttt{shadow=true} is rejected in this sparse path. Check consistency
with MD forces. With three PET blocks, the paper derives $K=7$, so the
configured \texttt{hops=3} is an approximation requiring convergence.

Truncated ASD does not simply zero distant blocks of a dense Hessian:
omitted couplings can share seeds with retained entries. For Cartesian mask
$\bm M_k$ and reconstructed $\widetilde{\bm H}_k$ before acoustic treatment,
\begin{align}
  \Delta\bm H_{\mathrm{disc}}&=-(\bm 1-\bm M_k)\odot\bm H,\\
  \Delta\bm H_{\mathrm{cont}}&=\bm M_k\odot(\widetilde{\bm H}_k-\bm H),
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
  \|\widetilde{\bm H}_k-\bm H\|_F^2
  =\|\Delta\bm H_{\mathrm{disc}}\|_F^2
   +\|\Delta\bm H_{\mathrm{cont}}\|_F^2.
\end{equation}
Here $\odot$ is entrywise multiplication. The paper finds contamination
tracking discarded norm down to roundoff; this is empirical, not a general
bound. Acoustic-sum-rule enforcement does not remove either error.

At $0.1\%$ heat-capacity tolerance at 300 K, most paper benchmarks converge
at two hops for MACE, three for PET-S, and four for PET-XS, with slower
zeolite cases \cite{sadMof}. Loaded MOF-5 requires its own comparison
against exact-pattern or feasible dense AD, inspecting signed modes and the
correction across temperatures. Convergence can be nonmonotonic near mode
thresholds. Periodic image folding also requires cell/supercell convergence.

\subsection{Normal modes and quantum harmonic heat capacity}
\label{sec:normal-modes}

Symmetrize the Hessian, enforce the acoustic sum rule, and mass weight:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_j H_{i\alpha,j\beta}=0,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  D_{i\alpha,j\beta}
  =\frac{H_{i\alpha,j\beta}}{\sqrt{m_i m_j}},
  \qquad
  \bm D\bm e_k=\omega_k^2\bm e_k.
\end{equation}
Uniform translations should have zero restoring force. Unlike the upstream
SADMOF observable, which maps negative eigenvalues to zero, the local
calculation preserves signed imaginary frequencies. Strict hybrid assembly
rejects unexpected imaginary modes and allows at most three translational
near-zero modes. Exploratory outputs instead discard modes at or below the
threshold (Section~\ref{sec:results-hessians}).

For retained positive modes, use the oscillator heat capacity derived in
Section~\ref{sec:quantum-statistical-mechanics}:
\begin{equation}
  x_k=\frac{\hbar\omega_k}{k_{\mathrm B}T}
      =\frac{h_{\mathrm P}c\tilde\nu_k}{k_{\mathrm B}T},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  C_{V,k}=k_{\mathrm B}
  \left[\frac{x_k/2}{\sinh(x_k/2)}\right]^2,
  \label{eq:quantum-harmonic-cv}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  c_V(T)=\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{cell}}}
  \sum_{k\in\mathcal V} C_{V,k},
  \label{eq:harmonic-cv}
\end{equation}
$\tilde\nu_k$ is wavenumber, and $M_{\mathrm{cell}}$ includes guests.
Using cell mass in grams and mode heat capacity in \si{J\per K} gives
\si{J\per g\per K}. Equivalently use $N_{\mathrm A}/M_{\mathrm{molar}}$.
One spectrum supplies the configured 100--\SI{500}{K} curve every
\SI{10}{K}; temperature changes populations, assuming fixed force constants.

\subsection{Optimized minima and the hybrid correction}
\label{sec:hybrid-method}

Relax the equilibrated empty structure directly at fixed cell for a separate
reference. For loaded systems, quench representative independent replicas
with the same MLIP and accept only force-converged, stable minima. Raw thermal
frames are not harmonic references. Current outputs use one geometry per
model/loading from \SI{400}{K}.

For $N_m$ accepted minima,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \overline{\Delta c^{\mathrm{har}}}(T)
  ={}&\frac{1}{N_m}\sum_{s=1}^{N_m}
  \Bigl[c_{\mathrm{qn},s}^{\mathrm{har}}(T)
  \\
  &\hspace{4em}-c_{\mathrm{cl},s}^{\mathrm{har}}\Bigr],
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:minimum-average}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  s_{\Delta c}^2(T)={}&\frac{1}{N_m-1}
  \sum_{s=1}^{N_m}\Bigl[
    \Delta c_s^{\mathrm{har}}(T)
  \\
  &-\overline{\Delta c^{\mathrm{har}}}(T)\Bigr]^2.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Use the same retained modes for quantum and classical terms, with
$k_{\mathrm B}$ per classical mode. Mixed host--guest motion requires the
complete loaded spectrum.
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  c_P^{\mathrm{approx}}(T)
  &=c_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{anh}}(T)
    +\overline{\Delta c^{\mathrm{har}}}(T),
  \\
  c_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{anh}}(T)
  &=\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{cell}}}\frac{\mathrm d\langle H\rangle_{\mathrm{cl}}}{\mathrm dT}.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:hybrid-cp}
\end{equation}
This combines an anharmonic classical $C_P$ with a harmonic $C_V$ correction,
assuming a small quantum pressure--volume term. Validation below about
\SI{200}{K} remains limited.

Persistent LLPR members must contribute both terms before their spread is taken:
\begin{align}
 \sigma_{\mathrm{model}}^2
  &=\sigma_{\mathrm{cl,model}}^2+\sigma_{\mathrm{har,model}}^2
    +2\operatorname{cov}_i(c_{P,\mathrm{cl}}^{(i)},\Delta c_{\mathrm{har}}^{(i)}),
    \label{eq:paired-model-uncertainty}\\
 u_{\mathrm{hyb}}^2
  &=\sigma_{\mathrm{MD}}^2+\sigma_{\mathrm{har,samp}}^2
    +\sigma_{\mathrm{model}}^2.
    \label{eq:combined-uncertainty}
\end{align}
The covariance is across matched model members. $\sigma_{\mathrm{MD}}$
is the sampling error of the central enthalpy derivative;
$\sigma_{\mathrm{har,samp}}$ comes from independently quenched structures.
Quadrature assumes independence of the sampling and model contributions.

Current single-replica analysis perturbs enthalpy means with Gaussian draws
using autocorrelation-based errors and recomputes the derivative. It supplies
within-trajectory error, not a block bootstrap or between-replica variance.
Bands omit the unestimated harmonic sampling term:
$u_{\mathrm{hyb}}=(\sigma_{\mathrm{MD}}^2+\sigma_{\mathrm{model}}^2)^{1/2}$.
LLPR Hessians use central-model geometries without member-specific relaxation.

heat-capacity difference; compressibility is also needed to quantify it.

At \SI{300}{K}, framework RMSDs are 0.659--\SI{0.725}{\angstrom},

Methane \SI{400}{ps} MSDs at \SI{300}{K} decrease with loading:
$8.9$--$9.2$, $5.1$--$5.6$, and $3.1$--$3.4\times10^3\,\text{\AA}^2$
for 50, 100, and 150 molecules, respectively. Guest radial distributions
peak near \SI{4}{\angstrom}. Mobility and crowding support the need for
anharmonic MD; a single harmonic geometry cannot describe diffusion.
Endpoint MSDs alone do not establish diffusion coefficients without a
verified diffusive regime, cell-motion control, and replica statistics.

Force convergence does not establish positive curvature: the two

\subsection{Uncertainty bands}
\label{sec:results-uncertainty}

Figures~\ref{fig:results-error-cea}--\ref{fig:results-error-gaussian}
separate MD sampling, classical LLPR, harmonic LLPR, and paired total
uncertainty. MD errors are about \SI{0.010}{J\per g\per K} at
\SI{300}{K}; model terms dominate. Covariance can make the total smaller
than an individual component (Eq.~\eqref{eq:paired-model-uncertainty}).

  \centering

  curves. Every panel uses all 8001 saved production frames at each

For PET-SOL with 150 guests at \SI{300}{K}, classical and harmonic CEA
spreads of 0.108 and \SI{0.155}{J\per g\per K} combine to
\SI{0.103}{J\per g\per K}, with inferred correlation $-0.75$.
PET-SOL/100 instead has correlation $+0.37$ and paired spread
\SI{0.153}{J\per g\per K}. Independent quadrature would miss both effects;
predictive spread is not reduced by $\sqrt{64}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/results-uncertainty-gaussian.png}
  \caption{Supplied Gaussian NPT/DPOSE uncertainty decomposition. Central
  hybrid curves, MD sampling errors, harmonic member corrections, and frame
  coverage are identical to the CEA comparison; only the classical member

  orange curves result from a different estimator and do not establish more

  \label{fig:results-error-gaussian}
\end{figure}

Gaussian propagation gives larger \SI{300}{K} bands in every case
(Table~\ref{tab:results-cp}), with positive classical--harmonic correlations
of 0.43--0.63. Smoother or narrower bands do not demonstrate calibration.
Recorded $\operatorname{var}(\beta\Delta U_i)$ spans about 2.4 to
$1.42\times10^3$, violating the small-variance CEA criterion; some direct
reweighting effective counts approach one. Neither collapsed reweighting
nor untested Gaussian tails validate these bands, whose coverage against
reference electronic-structure data remains unestablished.

The separate \texttt{variance-hybrid} calculation also changes the central
classical estimator. At \SI{300}{K}, hybrid differences from the derivative
route range from $-0.045$ to $+0.064\,\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$,
with MD errors of 0.022--\SI{0.030}{J\per g\per K} from squared-fluctuation
correlations. Shared trajectories and harmonic corrections make these
cross-checks correlated. Quantitative predictions require stable Hessians,
stationary independent trajectories, multiple quenched structures, and
validated member propagation.

\section{Conclusions}

PET-MAD and PET-SOL give exploratory room-temperature heat capacities rising
from about 0.93 to \SI{1.16}{J\per g\per K} with methane loading. Harmonic
quantization removes 59--63\% of the classical term; guest mobility supports
retaining anharmonic MD. Broad agreement with digitized reference trends
does not establish quantitative accuracy. Stable spectra, independent
trajectory/minimum sampling, and validated paired uncertainty propagation
are needed before interpreting these curves as converged predictions.
